# Study guide: present value and future value

MATH 118, lecture of 2026-08-26. Notes: `../notes/2026-08-26.md`.

## What this is for

Money at different points in time is not comparable. $1,000 today and $1,000 in fifteen years
are different amounts, because the $1,000 today can sit in a bank and grow. So any question
of the form "which offer is better?" is meaningless until both offers are moved to the same
point in time.

That is the entire job of these two formulas. They are a **time machine for money**.

## The two formulas are one formula

```
P = B · e^(−rt)        move money BACKWARD in time (discounting)
B = P · e^(rt)         move money FORWARD in time (compounding)
```

Do not memorize these as two separate facts. They are the same equation solved for different
letters. If you know one, divide or multiply both sides by `e^(rt)` and you have the other.
The only thing to remember is:

> **Going back in time, the exponent is negative. Going forward, it's positive.**

Everything else follows. If you ever forget which, sanity-check it: money in the past is
*worth less* than money in the future, so discounting must shrink the number, and `e^(negative)`
is less than 1. That check takes two seconds and it will save you on an exam.

| Symbol | Is | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| `P` | present value | now |
| `B` | future value | at time `t` |
| `r` | constant rate, as a decimal | — |
| `t` | years | — |

## Connects to

This is an application of the exponential function, so everything you know about `e^x` still
applies. Specifically, you will need `ln` the moment a problem asks for `r` or `t` instead of
an amount, because that is the only way to get a variable out of an exponent.

Expect this to come back when the course reaches integration. The continuous-payment version
(an income stream rather than a single lump sum) is the same idea with an integral wrapped
around it.

## Traps

1. **Percent vs decimal.** `r = 0.04`, never `4`. This is the single most common way to lose
   a whole problem, and the number comes out absurd so you can catch it.
2. **Sign of the exponent.** Backward is negative. See the sanity check above.
3. **`B` vs `V`.** He used both letters for future value in the same lecture. They are the
   same quantity. Do not assume a new symbol means a new concept.
4. **Lowercase `t`.** He self-corrected on the board.
5. **Comparing raw numbers.** If a problem gives you two offers at different dates and you
   compare the dollar amounts directly, you have not answered the question. Convert both first.
6. **Which direction to convert.** Both work. PV of everything, or FV of everything. What you
   cannot do is compare one PV against one FV. Pick a point in time and move *everything* to it.

## What a test question looks like

Four recognizable shapes:

1. **Direct.** Given three of `P`, `B`, `r`, `t`, find the fourth amount. Plug in.
2. **Comparison.** Two or three offers at different dates, "which should you take?" Convert
   all to present value, compare, state the winner *and* the margin.
3. **Backward.** Solve for `r` or `t`. Requires `ln`. Rearrange to isolate the exponential
   first, then take `ln` of both sides.
4. **Conceptual.** "Why is present value always less than future value when `r > 0`?" No
   arithmetic. Because `e^(−rt) < 1` for positive `r` and `t`, so discounting always shrinks.

## Memorize vs derive

**Memorize:** `P = B·e^(−rt)`. One formula.

**Derive:** everything else. The future value form is algebra. The `r` and `t` forms are one
`ln` away:

```
r = ln(B/P) / t          t = ln(B/P) / r
```

Both come from taking `ln` of `B/P = e^(rt)`. If you can do that step, you never need to
memorize them.
