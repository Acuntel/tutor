# Practice: present and future value

MATH 118, from the 2026-08-26 lecture. Assume continuous compounding at a constant rate
throughout. Round money to the cent.

**Attempt everything before scrolling to the solutions.**

---

## Problems

**1.** You are promised $5,000 in 8 years. At `r = 0.05`, what is that worth today?

**2.** You deposit $2,000 at `r = 0.035` and leave it for 12 years. How much is in the account?

**3.** Someone offers you a choice: **$8,000 today**, or **$10,000 in 5 years**. The rate is
`r = 0.04`. Which is better, and by how much in today's dollars?

**4.** You put in $1,000 and it grows to $1,500 in 6 years. What was the interest rate?

**5.** At `r = 0.04`, how long does it take for any amount of money to double?
(Notice the amount is not given. Should it matter?)

**6.** A contract pays you **$500 in 2 years** and **$700 in 5 years**. At `r = 0.045`, what
is the whole contract worth today?

**7.** Finish what the lecture ran out of time on. Option 2 is **$1,500 in two years**.
Option 3 is **$1,400 today** and a sympathy card. At `r = 0.04`, which wins, and by how much?

**8.** No arithmetic. Explain why present value is always *less* than future value whenever
`r > 0` and `t > 0`. Then explain what happens when `t = 0`, and why that makes sense.

## Solutions

**1.** Moving backward, so the exponent is negative.
```
P = 5000 · e^(−0.05 × 8) = 5000 · e^(−0.4) = 5000 × 0.670320 = $3,351.60
```

**2.** Moving forward, so the exponent is positive.
```
B = 2000 · e^(0.035 × 12) = 2000 · e^(0.42) = 2000 × 1.521962 = $3,043.92
```

**3.** You cannot compare $8,000 and $10,000 directly. Move the future payment back.
```
PV(option B) = 10000 · e^(−0.04 × 5) = 10000 · e^(−0.2) = $8,187.31
```
**$10,000 in 5 years wins**, by `8187.31 − 8000 = $187.31` in today's dollars.

**4.** Solve for `r`. Isolate the exponential, then take `ln`.
```
1500 = 1000 · e^(6r)
1.5  = e^(6r)
ln(1.5) = 6r
r = ln(1.5)/6 = 0.405465/6 = 0.067578 ≈ 6.76%
```
Check: `1000 · e^(0.067578 × 6) = $1,500.00` ✓

**5.** Doubling means `B = 2P`, so `P` cancels. **That is why the amount is not given, and it
does not matter** — the doubling time depends only on `r`.
```
2P = P · e^(0.04t)
2  = e^(0.04t)
ln(2) = 0.04t
t = 0.693147/0.04 = 17.33 years
```

**6.** Each payment gets discounted separately, then added. Present values are additive.
```
500 · e^(−0.045 × 2) = 500 × 0.913931 = $456.97
700 · e^(−0.045 × 5) = 700 × 0.798516 = $558.96
                              total    = $1,015.93
```

**7.**
```
PV(option 2) = 1500 · e^(−0.04 × 2) = 1500 · e^(−0.08) = 1500 × 0.923116 = $1,384.67
PV(option 3) = $1,400.00   (already today's money, no discounting needed)
```
**Option 3 wins by $15.33**, before valuing the sympathy card.

Caveat worth carrying: the lecture never restated the rate for this comparison. `r = 0.04`
is carried over from the earlier example. At a low enough rate option 2 would win, so if this
shows up on a problem set, check what rate you are given.

**8.** For `r > 0` and `t > 0`, the exponent `−rt` is negative, and `e^(negative) < 1`.
Multiplying the future amount by a number less than 1 shrinks it, so `P < B` always.

The intuition: money you have now can earn interest, so it takes *less* than $1,000 today to
become $1,000 later. Waiting has a cost, and the discount factor is that cost.

At `t = 0`: `e^(0) = 1`, so `P = B`. Money right now is worth exactly itself. There is no
waiting to compensate for. That is the correct behavior, and it is a good sanity check that
you have the formula the right way round.
