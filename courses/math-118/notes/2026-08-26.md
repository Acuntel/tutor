## The two formulas

Assume a **constant interest rate `r`**, compounded continuously. That assumption is the
basis of every formula in this course, and the instructor stated it explicitly.

**Present value.** How much you would need to put in the bank *now* to have a given amount
later:

```
P = B · e^(−rt)
```

**Future value.** Rearranged, how much you would *have* later if you deposit `P` now and
earn `r` for time `t`:

```
B = P · e^(rt)
```

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `P` | present value, the amount now |
| `B` | future value, the amount at time `t` |
| `r` | constant interest rate, as a decimal |
| `t` | time in years |

The two are exact inverses. Discounting by `e^(−rt)` and then compounding by `e^(rt)`
returns the original amount.

## What the formulas are *for*

The instructor's framing, which is the part worth remembering: this gives you a way of
**moving payments backward or forward in time** so that two payments offered at different
dates can be compared on equal footing.

To compare two offers, convert both to present value, or convert both to future value.
Either works. What you cannot do is compare a number today against a number in ten years
directly.

## Worked example from the lecture

Someone offers you $1,000 to be paid 15 years from now. At `r = 0.04`, what is that worth
today?

```
P = 1000 · e^(−0.04 × 15)
  = 1000 · e^(−0.6)
  = 1000 × 0.548812
  = $548.81
```

So in theory you should be willing to pay about **$548.81** today for that contract.
